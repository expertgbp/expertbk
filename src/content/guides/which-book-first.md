## "I have five ideas and no idea which one to write first."

If you're nodding along already, you're in good company: prolific, accomplished professionals land here constantly, and it's a very different kind of stuck than the blank-page kind most people picture when they think about writer's block.

This is a different problem than not having enough to say. If you're a prolific, accomplished professional, you probably have the opposite issue: a folder of notes for one book, a deck you keep meaning to turn into another, a story from your own life that a colleague keeps telling you to write down, and a fourth idea that came to you in the shower last week and won't leave.

Being frozen by too many good options isn't indecision. It's a real, common trap for people with real, deep expertise, and it has a real fix: a way to score the ideas against each other instead of trying to decide by mood, which changes daily.

## Why more ideas make this harder, not easier

It seems like having five ideas should be better than having one. In practice, it's often worse, because each idea keeps a little bit of your attention, and none of them ever gets the sustained focus a finished book actually requires. You start one, a shinier idea interrupts, you switch, momentum resets. Repeat that cycle for a year or two and you can end up with four unfinished first chapters and nothing published, despite having more than enough material for all four books.

The fix isn't picking harder with the same instincts that got you stuck. It's using a structured scoring method that takes the decision out of your mood on any given Tuesday.

## The five-factor scoring method

Score each of your book ideas from 1 to 5 on each factor below. Be honest, not hopeful. This isn't about which idea you love most today. It's about which idea is actually ready to become your first finished, published book.

Score honestly, in one sitting, and let the numbers do the deciding rather than reopening the debate afterward.

### 1. Reader clarity (1 to 5)

Can you name the one specific reader for this book in a single sentence, without hedging? A 5 means you can picture them clearly: their job, their exact frustration, the moment they'd pick up your book. A 1 means the best you can do is "professionals in my industry" or "people who want to improve." The sharper the reader, the higher the score, because reader clarity predicts almost everything else that follows: structure, positioning, and eventually, sales.

### 2. Promise clarity (1 to 5)

Can you state, in one sentence, what this book gives the reader that they didn't have before? A 5 means the promise is specific and different from what's already on the shelf. A 1 means the promise is really just a topic ("a book about leadership") rather than an outcome ("how a new manager runs their first hard conversation without losing the team's trust").

### 3. Material readiness (1 to 5)

How much of the actual content already exists in some form, talks you've given, frameworks you use with clients, notes you've kept for years? A 5 means you could talk through most of this book right now, off the top of your head, for an hour without notes. A 1 means you'd be researching and developing new material from scratch.

### 4. Business or life fit (1 to 5)

Does this book connect directly to where your career, practice, or life is heading right now? A 5 means the book supports what you're already building: speaking, consulting, a practice, a next chapter. A 1 means the book is a total departure from anything else you're currently doing, which isn't disqualifying, but it does mean less momentum carries over from your existing work and relationships.

### 5. Energy (1 to 5)

Be honest about which idea you actually want to spend the next several months living inside. A 5 means you feel a pull toward this one even when you imagine the hardest parts of writing it. A 1 means you'd be doing it mostly out of obligation. This factor exists on purpose, because a technically perfect idea you dread working on rarely gets finished either.

## Why this trap hits prolific professionals specifically

This isn't a random problem. It shows up disproportionately among a specific kind of person: the accomplished professional with twenty or thirty years of expertise, multiple areas of genuine authority, and enough material for several books, not because they're indecisive by nature, but because they actually have that much to say. CEOs, consultants, and senior specialists tend to be the ones stuck here most often, precisely because a career full of real accomplishment tends to generate a career full of real book ideas.

If this is you, it's worth naming the upside directly: having too many ideas is a much better position than having none, even though it doesn't feel that way while you're stuck choosing. The scoring method exists because this specific kind of person needs a decision tool, not a longer list of reasons the ideas are all worthy. They probably are all worthy. Only one of them gets to be first.

## Common mistakes when choosing a first book

**Picking based on what seems most "marketable."** Chasing whatever topic seems trendiest right now often means picking the idea you're least equipped to finish quickly, because trend-chasing pulls you toward material readiness score of 1 or 2. A book you can actually finish well beats a trendier book you abandon at chapter four.

**Waiting for the "perfect" idea to reveal itself.** There often isn't a single obviously correct answer sitting there waiting to be noticed. Several of your ideas may genuinely be strong enough to succeed. The scoring method isn't about finding the one true best idea. It's about finding the one that's most ready right now.

**Letting the newest idea win by default.** New ideas feel exciting precisely because they're new and untested by the disappointment of hard work. That excitement is real, but it's not the same as readiness. This is exactly why the energy factor is weighed alongside four other factors instead of allowed to decide alone.

**Trying to combine multiple ideas into one book.** Cramming a methodology book, a memoir, and a leadership framework into a single manuscript because you can't choose usually produces an unfocused book that serves none of the three ideas well. Positioning requires one reader and one promise. Combining ideas at the outline stage is one of the fastest ways to end up back in the write-rewrite loop covered in our guide on finishing a stuck draft.

## How book type interacts with the scoring

The five factors apply regardless of book type, but book type does affect how you'll likely score certain factors. A business or leadership book idea often scores well on material readiness if you've taught the content in talks or client work for years. A memoir idea often scores lower on material readiness even when the story is vivid, simply because a lived story hasn't been shaped into a book structure yet, and higher on energy, since personal stories often pull hardest at attention. A self-help book idea lives or dies on promise clarity, since the category is crowded and a vague promise gets lost fastest there.

None of this should override your honest scores. It's context for why two ideas of similar quality might land at different totals, and why that's expected rather than a sign something's wrong with the method.

## How to use the scores

Add up the five numbers for each idea. The highest total is usually your answer. If there's a near tie, look specifically at reader clarity and material readiness first: those two factors predict how smoothly the actual writing process goes more than any of the others.

If your highest-scoring idea isn't the one you expected, sit with that for a day before overriding it. The scoring method exists precisely because unaided instinct, in this specific situation (too many good options, no external pressure to choose), tends to default to whichever idea felt most exciting most recently, not necessarily the one most ready to become a finished book.

If your highest-scoring idea and your gut genuinely disagree, that's useful information too. It often means the exciting idea needs its promise sharpened before it's really ready, not that the method failed. Give it one more pass at sharpening the promise and rescoring before you decide the numbers were wrong; more often than not, the gap closes once the promise gets specific enough.

## What to do if you can't bring yourself to trust a score

Some professionals fill out the scorecard, get a clear winner, and still feel a pull to second-guess it. If that's you, try this: write down the single biggest reason you'd override the top score. Usually it's one of a few things: fear that the highest-scoring idea is "too simple" to be a real book, worry that a different idea would impress people more, or a nagging feeling that the exciting new idea deserves a chance before it fades.

None of those reasons are about which book is most ready to be finished well. They're about status, novelty, or fear, three things the scoring method was built specifically to route around. That doesn't mean the override is always wrong. It means it's worth naming the real reason before you act on it, instead of dressing up a status worry as a business decision.

## A worked example

Say you're a consultant with three ideas: a book on your core methodology, a memoir about a career pivot, and a leadership book aimed at first-time managers.

The methodology book might score a 5 on material readiness (you teach this constantly) and a 4 on business fit (it directly supports your consulting practice), but only a 3 on reader clarity if you haven't yet narrowed "businesses" down to a specific type of leader. The memoir might score a 5 on energy (you've wanted to write this for years) but a 2 on material readiness (you haven't actually drafted much of it yet) and a 3 on promise clarity (the arc isn't fully mapped). The leadership book might score a 4 across reader clarity, promise clarity, and business fit, with a 3 on energy, since it's solid but not the one keeping you up at night.

Totaled, the methodology book and the leadership book likely edge out the memoir for a strong first choice, not because the memoir is a bad idea, but because it isn't the most ready one yet. That doesn't mean the memoir never gets written. It means it waits for its turn, with a clearer path, once the first book is done and that specific expertise has been tested.

## What to do with the ideas you don't pick

This is the part that makes the whole exercise feel less like a loss. The ideas that score lower aren't rejected. They're sequenced. Write them down, with their scores and the specific gaps that held them back (material readiness, reader clarity, whatever it was), and set them aside on purpose rather than trying to hold all of them in your head while you write the first one.

Most prolific professionals who go through this exercise do eventually write more than one book. The scoring method isn't about narrowing your ambition. It's about giving your first book an actual finish line instead of permanent competition from three other unfinished drafts.
