answerBlock: >-
  A legacy book gets written around your real deadline, not ours: an age, an anniversary, a promise made
  to a grandchild. The process uses simple, low-tech extraction sessions so decades of memory and
  perspective get captured in your own words, even if you've never used a computer for anything more than
  email. Editing, publishing, and launch follow, and you keep 100% of your rights and royalties, on your
  timeline.
whoWritesThis: >-
  Late-career and late-life authors write these: a grandparent who wants grandchildren to know a full life,
  not just the last twenty years of it, a family honoring someone who's gone, a person who's watched enough
  birthdays pass to know this one might be the deadline that matters. GBP has had authors start their first
  book at 76 and at 85.
painPoints:
  - objection: "Is it too late for me to do this?"
    reframe: >-
      No, and this question comes up more than almost any other. Late-life debuts are common, not rare, and
      a life lived longer is a life with more to say, not less.
  - objection: "I don't know how to use a computer well enough to write a book."
    reframe: >-
      You don't need to. Extraction sessions are built around talking, not typing. Many legacy authors
      dictate their story out loud in guided sessions and never touch a keyboard for a single chapter.
  - objection: "Whose voice should this be in? Mine, or written about someone I'm honoring?"
    reframe: >-
      This gets decided early and deliberately, not guessed at. A book can be written in your own first
      person, or carefully built to honor someone else's voice and memory if you're writing on their behalf.
  - objection: "My deadline is real. I don't have years to finish this."
    reframe: >-
      Then the calendar is built around that deadline, not around ours. Your age, your anniversary, or your
      promise sets the pace here; there's no countdown timer trying to rush you, only a real date that
      matters to you.
processDifferences: >-
  A legacy book is paced against a real personal deadline rather than a marketing calendar, and it never
  uses a cart-style countdown to move things along. Extraction favors spoken storytelling over typed drafts,
  since many legacy authors are more comfortable talking through a life than writing one. Perspective is
  decided explicitly up front: your own voice, or a careful, honoring account written for someone who can no
  longer tell it themselves.
