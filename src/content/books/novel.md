  A novel gets finished by breaking the write-rewrite loop on purpose, with real developmental deadlines

painPoints:
  - objection: "I've rewritten chapter three so many times I've lost count. I don't think I'll ever finish."
    reframe: >-
      One of our own authors spent a decade in that exact loop. It's usually a sign of writing and editing
      at the same time with no outside deadline. Developmental sessions with real checkpoints are what
      break it, not more willpower alone.
  - objection: "I've queried dozens of agents and gotten nothing but rejections or silence."
    reframe: >-
      Agent rejection is close to universal in fiction, even for books that later sell well; it's a brutal,
      crowded gate, not a verdict on your writing. Partnership publishing is a real path to print that
      doesn't run through that same gate.
  - objection: "What if I finish this and no one likes it?"
    reframe: >-
      That fear doesn't fully go away, and we won't pretend it does. What helps is real developmental
      feedback before publication, from readers who tell you honestly what's working and what isn't, instead
      of finding out only after it's in print.
  - objection: "I don't want to lose creative control over my own story."
    reframe: >-
      You won't. You keep 100% of your rights, and editing here means shaping plot and pacing with you, not
      rewriting your voice for you. The story stays yours at every stage.
processDifferences: >-
  Fiction skips the "one reader, one promise" positioning used for expert nonfiction and instead positions
  by genre, comparable titles, and audience expectations for that genre. Editing is developmental first,
  working on plot structure, character arcs, and pacing, rather than extracting an expert's argument from
  spoken sessions. Publication happens outside the traditional query-and-wait system that shelves so many
  finished manuscripts, with real deadlines that finally end the private rewrite loop.

    answer: >-
