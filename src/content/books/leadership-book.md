painPoints:
  - objection: "Leadership has been written about a thousand times. What's left to say?"
    reframe: >-
      Almost everything general has been said. What hasn't been said is your specific version of it: the
      exact framework you used the week three people quit at once, or the model you built for managing
      remote teams no book had covered yet. Positioning narrows the promise to what only you can teach.
  - objection: "I've led teams for 20 years, but I've never written a page in my life."
    reframe: >-
      Leading and writing are different skills, and you only need one of them here. Structured extraction
      sessions are built around talking through decisions you've already made a hundred times. The writing
      comes from the system, not a blank page staring back at you.
  - objection: "I don't want this to read like a business school textbook."
    reframe: >-
      Then it won't. The best leadership books read like a mentor talking straight to you, not a
      case-study binder. Your stories, your language, and your specific mistakes stay in, because that's
      what a reader remembers and repeats to their own team.
  - objection: "I want people to actually use this in training, not just read it once."
    reframe: >-
      That's a structure decision made early. A book meant for training gets built with named frameworks,
      repeatable exercises, and chapter takeaways a manager can hand to a new hire, not just a narrative arc
      meant to be read once and shelved.
processDifferences: >-
  A leadership book leans harder on turning lived experience into a teachable model than a general
  nonfiction extraction does. Positioning identifies the one leadership moment or problem the book owns.
  Extraction sessions are built to surface a named framework, real scenarios, and reusable exercises, not
  just chronological stories, so the finished manuscript can be assigned in a training program as easily as
  read on a flight.
