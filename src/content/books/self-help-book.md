  A self-help book stands out when it gives a reader something to do, not just something to feel better

whoWritesThis: >-
  Coaches, therapists, and health coaches write most of these: people who have walked clients through a
  change process enough times to know the steps cold, even if they've never written them down in order.
  This is the book for the person whose clients keep asking, "is there something I can read between our
  sessions?"
painPoints:
  - objection: "The self-help shelf is enormous. Mine will just disappear into it."
    reframe: >-
      Most self-help books disappear because they promise everything to everyone. Positioning narrows this
      to one reader and one specific change, so your book is the one that actually names their exact
      problem instead of a general version of it.
  - objection: "I know how to coach someone through this in person. I don't know how to write it as steps."
    reframe: >-
      That translation is exactly what structured extraction is built for. You talk through how you'd guide
      a real client through the exact moment they're stuck, and the sessions turn that into a repeatable
      structure a reader can follow alone.
  - objection: "I don't want to overpromise. My clients change, but I can't guarantee anyone else will."
    reframe: >-
      Good instinct, and it's the right one. The book teaches the method honestly, with real process and
      real limits, rather than promising an outcome no one can guarantee. That honesty is also what builds
      trust with a skeptical reader.
  - objection: "How much of my own story do I put in versus just teaching the method?"
    reframe: >-
      Enough to prove you've lived it, not so much that the book becomes a memoir. Positioning decides that
      balance early: your story earns the reader's trust, the method does the actual work.
processDifferences: >-
  A self-help book needs a teachable method with steps a reader can act on alone, which is a different job
  than a standard nonfiction extraction focused on argument and authority. Extraction sessions are built to
  surface concrete exercises, checklists, and decision points, tested against how you already walk a real
  client through change, so the finished book works as a companion, not just a read.

faqs:
  - question: "Do I need a formal, named methodology to write a self-help book?"
    answer: >-
      It helps, but most coaches already have one that's never been written down as a named process. The
      Engineer stage is usually where that process gets named and structured for the first time.
  - question: "Can I include client stories?"
    answer: >-
      Yes, with names changed or details composited so no client is identifiable. This is handled explicitly
      during editing, with the same care you already use with clients.
  - question: "Will this book make income promises for readers?"
    answer: >-
      No. The book teaches an honest method and real process. It never promises a specific outcome, because
      no book can guarantee what a reader will do with it.
  - question: "How is this different from a memoir?"
    answer: >-
      A memoir is built around your story as the spine. A self-help book uses your story as evidence, and
      the method itself, the steps and exercises, is the spine.
