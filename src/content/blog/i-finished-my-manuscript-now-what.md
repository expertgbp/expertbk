A finished manuscript is closer to the halfway point than the finish line, and that's good news, not bad news: the hardest, most uncertain stage is behind you. What's left is a known sequence, not a mystery. Structural and line editing come first, then cover and interior design, then metadata and distribution setup, then a real launch plan built around a specific date. Most authors move from finished manuscript to published, promoted book in 3 to 5 months.

## Step 1: Developmental editing

Before anything else, a developmental editor reads for structure: does the argument hold together, does the story arc land, is anything missing that the reader needs. This is the step most first-time authors underestimate, because the manuscript already feels finished to the person who wrote it.

## Step 2: Line and copy editing

Sentence-level work: clarity, rhythm, grammar, and making sure your voice survives the process instead of getting flattened into something generic. Ask to see a sample edit. If it sounds like someone else wrote it, that's the wrong editor.

## Step 3: Positioning check

Even if positioning happened before writing started, it gets revisited here. Does the finished manuscript still match the one reader and one promise it was built around? Small drifts happen during drafting, and this is the last checkpoint to catch them before design locks the book in place.

## Step 4: Cover design

A cover has one job: to make the right reader stop scrolling. This isn't decoration. It's the single highest-leverage design decision in the entire process, tested against the genre shelf the book actually competes on.

## Step 5: Interior design and formatting

Typesetting, chapter breaks, front and back matter, and formatting for both print and digital editions. Small details here, like a cramped page or an inconsistent header, are the fastest way to make a professionally written book look amateur.

## Step 6: ISBN, metadata, and distribution setup

Getting the book listed correctly everywhere books are sold: the right categories, the right keywords, the right metadata so the book is findable by the reader it was written for, not buried under a thousand similar titles.

## Step 7: Advance copies and early reviews

Real reviews take time to accumulate, and a launch without any is a launch without social proof. Advance reader copies go out before the public launch date specifically to build that early base.

## Step 8: The launch plan

A specific date, a sequence of announcements, and a plan for the first two weeks, not just the first day. This is the step most likely to be skipped by authors who publish alone, and it's the reason so many well-written books quietly sell under 100 copies.

## Step 9: What happens after launch week

A book isn't an event. It's an asset that needs quarterly pushes: speaking opportunities, media, courses, and continued promotion months after the launch date has passed. Authors who stop at step 8 are the ones who come back later asking why the book isn't selling.
