obstacles:
  - objection: "Parents don't have time to read."
    reframe: >-
      Then the book has to fit the time they have, not the time you wish they had. Positioning and
      structure build it in short, usable pieces from the start, so one chapter at 11 p.m. is enough to
      change tomorrow.
  - objection: "Every family is different; how can one book help?"
    reframe: >-
      True, and it's also true that the same few moments (bedtime, the meltdown in public, the sibling
      fight) show up in nearly every home. The book teaches the pattern underneath the moment, and each
      parent adapts the specifics themselves.
  - objection: "I worry about being judged for my own parenting."
    reframe: >-
      You don't have to present yourself as a parent who never struggles. The most trusted parenting
      books admit the hard nights too. Positioning decides how much of your own story to include, and
      editing keeps it honest without turning into oversharing.

    answer: >-

  - question: "Should I include my own family in the book?"
    answer: >-
      Only as much as you're comfortable with, and never more. Many parenting experts include a few real,
      low-stakes moments from their own home for warmth, then lean on composite client examples for the
      harder lessons. Positioning decides the mix before a word is written.
  - question: "What if my focus is narrow, like only toddlers or only teens?"
    answer: >-
      Narrow is often the advantage. A precise book for one age and stage outperforms a vague book for
      every parent of every child. Positioning decides this before a word is written.
